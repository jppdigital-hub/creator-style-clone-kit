# RS-002: Creator Style Clone

Version: 1.0
ResourceStak: Creator Style Clone
Status: Packaged pilot

## What this is

This is a way to study how a creator makes content people notice, watch, remember, and act on.

You're looking for parts of the work you can understand and maybe adapt. You're not copying the creator's biography, stories, opinions, worldview, catchphrases, humor, appearance, or voice.

The point is to work out what job each part of the content is doing, then find a version of that job that makes sense for you.

## The question

> How does this creator choose what to make, get people to notice it, keep them watching, and give them a useful next step?

That question is broad on purpose. A list of titles and thumbnails won't tell you how the pieces fit together.

## Evidence labels

Use one of these labels whenever you write an important claim.

### OBSERVED

You can point to it in the sample. You saw the title pattern, opening, visual, CTA, or repeated structure yourself.

### REPORTED

The creator or somebody on the team said it about the process. You didn't verify the internal claim yourself.

### INFERENCE

This is your reading of what the evidence seems to suggest. The creator didn't say it directly.

### ADAPTATION

This is a mechanism you might change and use in your own work. It isn't a claim about the creator.

### UNKNOWN

You couldn't support the claim from the material you had. Leave it open. Don't fill the blank with a confident guess.

## The six parts

### 1. What do they keep making?

Start with the ideas before you study the presentation.

Look for:

- recurring topics
- recurring audience problems
- recurring promises
- evergreen and timely subjects
- search-driven and recommendation-driven ideas
- beginner and advanced subjects
- commercial intent
- proof-of-work ideas
- recurring series
- winners and ordinary or underperforming pieces

Ask:

> What kinds of problems does this creator keep deciding are worth making something about?

Write down the pattern you can support. Don't confuse a familiar format with an idea-selection system. A creator can use the same format for very different subjects, and the subject still matters.

### 2. How do they make the idea easy to notice?

Study how the idea gets turned into something a person can understand quickly.

Look at:

- titles
- thumbnails
- the opening promise
- specificity
- curiosity
- visible outcomes
- numbers and time frames
- beginner access
- first-person proof
- recurring title or thumbnail shells
- how much work gets compressed into the promise

For each pattern, record:

- an example
- how often it appears in your sample
- the job it might be doing
- when it seems to work
- when it seems to fail
- whether it depends on the creator's identity

Don't call a pattern durable because you found it once.

### 3. How does each piece unfold?

Follow the piece from the first few seconds to the last line. Pay attention to what happens when.

Look at:

- the first 15 to 60 seconds
- whether the promise gets restated
- when proof appears
- how the problem gets set up
- how much context the piece gives
- the balance of teaching and story
- demonstrations
- section order
- pacing
- examples
- objections
- recap
- ending
- CTA timing

Ask:

> What does the creator make me understand first, and what do they save until later?

Write down the sequence before you explain why it might work.

### 4. What job are the visuals doing?

Study visuals by the job they do, not as decoration.

Look for:

- talking-head sections
- screen recordings
- live builds
- diagrams
- slides
- B-roll
- charts
- captions
- overlays
- zooms
- proof screenshots
- cut frequency
- attention resets

For each recurring visual device, ask:

> What problem is this visual solving?

It may prove something, explain something, shorten a complicated idea, bring attention back, help the viewer stay oriented, build trust, or create pace.

If you can't tell what a device is doing, mark your explanation `UNKNOWN` or `INFERENCE`.

### 5. Where does the audience go next?

Content usually points somewhere. Follow the path instead of treating the CTA like an afterthought.

Look at:

- the main next action
- CTA placement
- free resources
- newsletters
- communities
- products or services
- sponsors and affiliates
- related content
- links in the description
- pinned comments
- end screens

Give the viewer one clear next thing to do. A creator can have more than one offer. A single piece still needs a clear next move.

That doesn't mean a creator can have only one offer. It means one piece should make the next useful move clear.

### 6. What can you actually tell about how it's made?

Study the production process only as far as the evidence allows.

Look for:

- research
- ideation
- scripting
- filming
- editing
- thumbnail production
- publishing cadence
- repurposing
- templates
- automation
- delegation
- quality control

Separate what the creator has confirmed from what you think might be happening. Don't invent a team, a software stack, a hidden analytics system, or a reason a piece succeeded.

## Style DNA and identity

The point of this framework is adaptation, not imitation.

### Style DNA, which may be adaptable

These are examples of mechanics you might be able to translate:

- proof appears early
- the title promises a finished capability
- a visual demonstration carries part of the explanation
- one next action is clear
- a successful content shell returns across different subjects
- the piece leaves behind a useful artifact

Call something `ADAPTATION` when you're talking about what you might do.

### Identity, which you should leave alone

Don't clone:

- biography
- personal stories
- opinions
- worldview
- signature phrases
- vocal mannerisms
- humor
- appearance
- persona
- private material

The adaptation question is:

> What job is this mechanism doing, and what could do the same job in my work?

## Build a sample you can trust

An ideal sample includes 10 to 20 representative pieces, with:

- a clear date range
- the platforms you studied
- several strong performers
- several ordinary or underperforming pieces
- titles and thumbnails
- transcripts or full text when available
- descriptions and CTAs
- linked resources
- creator interviews or behind-the-scenes material when available

The sample doesn't need to be perfect. It does need to be clear enough that somebody else can understand what you looked at.

## The workflow

### 1. Define the question

Name the creator, the sample, and the thing you're trying to understand.

### 2. Record observations first

Write down what is visible before you explain what it means.

### 3. Work through the six parts

Keep idea selection, packaging, structure, visuals, conversion, and production separate. They affect each other, but they're different variables.

### 4. Look for repetition

Separate patterns that keep showing up from one-off choices. A pattern needs more than one example.

### 5. Build the profile

Turn your notes into a short Creator Style Profile. Keep confidence and open questions visible.

### 6. Separate mechanics from identity

Make two lists, one for the parts you could translate and one for the parts you should leave alone.

### 7. Make your adaptation map

For each useful mechanism, write what job it performs and how you could perform that job in your own way.

## Standard deliverables

A finished study should contain:

1. A one-sentence style thesis.
2. An Idea Selection profile.
3. A Packaging pattern library.
4. A Content Architecture map.
5. A Visual Language map.
6. A Conversion Architecture map.
7. A Production Machine map.
8. A Style DNA list.
9. An Identity and Do Not Clone list.
10. A comparison of strong, ordinary, and underperforming pieces.
11. Reusable content shells.
12. An adaptation map for the target creator.
13. A list of `UNKNOWN` claims and open questions.
14. A source and sample list.

## Final integrity check

Before you call the study finished, check the following:

- Every important observation points back to a sample or source.
- Reported process claims are labeled `REPORTED`.
- Interpretations are labeled `INFERENCE`.
- Adaptations are labeled `ADAPTATION`.
- Unsupported internal details stay `UNKNOWN`.
- Winners and ordinary pieces are both represented when possible.
- You haven't confused topic selection with presentation style.
- You haven't invented a reason a piece succeeded.
- You haven't copied third-party text, personal stories, or identity markers.
- The next action is clear for each piece you studied.
